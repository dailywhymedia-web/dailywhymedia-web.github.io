How to choose a tree for your USDA hardiness zone

November 18, 2026 · Daily Why Media

Photo: Nennieinszweidrei on Pixabay

The best tree for your yard starts with one question: will it survive your winters? Your USDA hardiness zone answers that, and it takes about a minute to find. It is only the first filter, though. Here is how to use it, plus a starter list for each part of the country.

Find your zone

Enter your ZIP code on the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map. The 2023 map groups places by their average coldest night of the year, using weather station data from 1991 to 2020. Each zone covers 10°F, and the lower the number, the colder the winter.

Plan for your own yard. USDA notes that the map is a general guide. Cities can run warmer, higher ground can run colder, and small microclimates may not show up on the map.

What your zone does not tell you

A zone is about winter cold. A tree can be rated for your zone and still struggle with your summer heat, rainfall, soil or space. Before you buy, check these four things:

  • Mature size. Measure the room it will need overhead and away from buildings and lines.
  • Sun. Match the tree to the light the spot really gets.
  • Soil and water. Some trees want wet ground, while others need sharp drainage.
  • Local advice. Your county extension office can flag pests, and species that spread too easily in your area.

Trees to start with, by zone

These picks come from the checked plant list behind our The Tree Atlas videos. Each one is typically rated for every zone in its group.

Zones 3 and 4: long, hard winters

Paper birchBright white peeling bark that stands out all winter.
Bur oakHuge acorns and thick bark that survives prairie fires.
Sugar mapleBrilliant fall color and the source of maple syrup.
White spruceA tough evergreen windbreak that shrugs off deep cold.

Zones 5 and 6: cold winters, warm summers

Northern red oakFast-growing for an oak, with deep red fall leaves.
Eastern redbudPink-purple flowers cover bare branches in early spring.
Black gumSome of the most reliable scarlet fall color of any tree.
River birchPeeling cinnamon bark, and happy in wet soil.

Zone 7: mild winters

Flowering dogwoodShowy white spring blooms and red berries for birds.
Japanese mapleDelicate lacy leaves in red, orange or green.
Southern magnoliaHuge fragrant white flowers and glossy evergreen leaves.
Crape myrtleMonths of summer flowers and smooth peeling bark.

Zones 8 and 9: short, gentle winters

Southern live oakHuge spreading limbs and leaves that stay green all year.
Bald cypressA conifer that drops its needles and can grow in standing water.
Sweetbay magnoliaLemon-scented flowers and silvery leaf undersides.
Desert willowOrchid-like flowers on very little water.

Before you order a tree online

  • Confirm that the listing's zone range includes yours.
  • Check whether the seller ships to your state, and when it ships.
  • Read the plant warranty, including what proof it requires.
  • Have the planting spot chosen before the box arrives.
